\begin{abstract}
libMesh is an open-source C++ library for parallel, spatially adaptive finite element and finite
volume simulation. Over two decades of development, the library has added
hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin and other statically condensable discretizations; isogeometric
analysis with rational bases and arbitrary B\'ezier extraction operators for trimmed geometry;
native polyhedral elements; and TIMPI, a standalone parallel communication submodule implementing
modern nonblocking-exchange algorithms. Automatic differentiation, certified reduced-basis model
order reduction, and adjoint-driven $hp$-adaptivity round out the expanded numerical toolkit. This
article surveys these capabilities and the multiphysics applications they enable.
\end{abstract}

\item Motivation and significance

libMesh is an open-source C++ library providing general, physics-independent infrastructure for
parallel, spatially adaptive finite element -- and, increasingly, finite volume -- simulation. Its
2006 description~\cite{kirk2006libmesh} covered a mesh and degree-of-freedom data model,
$h$-adaptive refinement and coarsening with hanging-node constraints, and a domain-decomposition
parallel model built atop third-party solver libraries such as PETSc. That core design still
holds; this article describes the capabilities added since.

libMesh has been under continuous development for the past twenty years, accumulating tens of
thousands of commits from a broad developer community. Its applications now require
discretizations and geometric representations unavailable in 2006: hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin formulations for general transport problems; isogeometric meshes
derived from trimmed or piecewise-rational-spline CAD geometry; and general polyhedral cells, which
reduce the mesh non-orthogonality that degrades finite volume flux approximations on tetrahedral
meshes. The parallel communication layer has also been substantially
reworked, and new infrastructure has been added for automatic differentiation, certified
reduced-order modeling, and goal-oriented adaptivity.

Several largely independent application communities motivate and use these capabilities. The
MOOSE multiphysics framework~\cite{harbour2025moose}, developed at Idaho National Laboratory
(INL), relies on libMesh for its finite element capabilities and, through the discontinuous
discretizations, polyhedral elements, and ghosting infrastructure described in Section 2, for its
finite volume capabilities. MOOSE underpins INL's fission and fusion reactor multiphysics work; an additional
MOOSE-based fusion-device effort is under way in the UK, where the Hartree Centre (STFC)
collaborates closely with the UK Atomic Energy Authority. Independently of MOOSE, Akselos builds
commercial reduced-order-modeling and digital-twin engineering-analysis products directly on
libMesh's certified reduced-basis infrastructure, and the IBAMR immersed boundary
framework~\cite{griffith2017ibfe} uses libMesh for its finite element representations of
immersed structures, both volumetric elastic bodies and surfaces. This article surveys the library-level capabilities underlying these
communities.

\item Software architecture

libMesh is organized around a small set of deliberately physics-independent core abstractions:
the mesh (\texttt{MeshBase}, composed of \texttt{Node} and \texttt{Elem} objects),
\texttt{DofMap}, \texttt{System}, and the finite element classes (\texttt{FEAbstract} and its
derived class template \texttt{FEGenericBase}, instantiated as \texttt{FEBase} and
\texttt{FEVectorBase} for scalar- and vector-valued families). An
application supplies the equations; libMesh supplies mesh management, degree-of-freedom
numbering, adaptivity, and parallel communication. The capabilities described below extend this
architecture: new finite element families, element types, and mesh constructs plug into the
existing \texttt{FEAbstract} and \texttt{Elem} hierarchies;
new solver and preconditioning machinery plugs into the existing \texttt{System}/\texttt{DofMap}
interfaces; and new parallel primitives are exposed through the bundled TIMPI submodule.

\item New software functionalities

\textbf{Hybridized discretizations and static condensation.} Users build hybridized schemes from
compatible finite element families: discontinuous element-interior spaces (e.g.,
\texttt{L2\_LAGRANGE}, \texttt{L2\_LAGRANGE\_VEC}, \texttt{L2\_RAVIART\_THOMAS}) for a primal field
and, in mixed formulations, its gradient or flux, and the \texttt{SIDE\_HIERARCHIC} family, whose
degrees of freedom live only on element sides, for the field's trace. Interior-penalty HDG
schemes need only the primal field and its trace. These family combinations support hybridized
Raviart--Thomas and hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) discretizations for general transport
problems, including the flow problems modeled by the MOOSE
\texttt{navier\_stokes} module. The \texttt{StaticCondensation},
\texttt{StaticCondensationDofMap}, and \texttt{StaticCondensationPreconditioner} classes then make
such schemes efficient: they assemble the full elemental system and eliminate element-interior
degrees of freedom through a local Schur complement before the global solve, using Eigen for the
local dense factorizations. Applications may exempt selected variables from condensation, leaving
them in the global system alongside the trace unknowns.

\textbf{Polygonal and polyhedral elements.} General polygonal and polyhedral cells are supported
natively through the \texttt{C0Polygon} and \texttt{C0Polyhedron} classes, whose topology (number
of sides, edges, and nodes) is determined at run time; libMesh's traditional elements fix their
topology at compile time. Polyhedral cells benefit finite volume discretizations over tetrahedral cells in two ways.
First, on a cell with many faces, the vector joining adjacent cell centroids tends to align more
closely with the normal of their shared face, reducing the non-orthogonality corrections that
two-point flux approximations require, and tends to pass closer to the face centroid, reducing
the skewness error of interpolating cell values to the face. Second, each cell has more face
neighbors than a tetrahedron's four, giving a larger, better-conditioned stencil for gradient
reconstruction. Polygonal and polyhedral cells participate in the rest of the mesh infrastructure
like any other element: neighbor finding, element-quality metrics, conversion to triangles and
tetrahedra, checkpoint/restart, ExodusII (\texttt{NSIDED}/\texttt{NFACED}) output, and VTU input
all support them.

\textbf{Ghosting.} The ghosting-functor framework (\texttt{GhostingFunctor}) lets applications
declare exactly which off-processor elements each processor requires at three nested levels:
coupling (matrix sparsity), algebraic (solution values), and geometric (mesh). Each level must be a
superset of the one before it: elements coupled to a processor's elements must be evaluable there,
and evaluable elements must be geometrically ghosted there. By default, each \texttt{DofMap}
uses \texttt{DefaultCoupling} instances for both its coupling and algebraic ghosting; these reach
side neighbors, including across periodic boundaries, out to a configurable number of layers.
Together with libMesh's discontinuous finite element spaces, this provides the face-neighbor
stencils of cell-centered finite volume discretizations, and additional layers provide the wider
stencils of higher-order reconstructions.
Specialized functors handle other coupling patterns: \texttt{PointNeighborCoupling} for elements
sharing any point; \texttt{OverlapCoupling} for elements that overlap geometrically without
topological connection, as in immersed methods; \texttt{NonManifoldGhostingFunctor} for meshes in
which more than two elements share a side, as in truss and shell-stiffener models; and
\texttt{SiblingCoupling} for coupling active elements to all descendants of their parent, as
needed when projecting onto parent elements for error indication.

\textbf{Isogeometric analysis.} The \texttt{RATIONAL\_BERNSTEIN} finite element family reweights
Bernstein/B\'ezier shape functions with per-node NURBS weights, giving the rational basis used in
isogeometric analysis (IGA)~\cite{hughes2005iga}. A corresponding element mapping type applies the
same rational representation to the geometric map itself, so meshes derived from
piecewise-rational-spline CAD geometry are represented exactly. Arbitrary B\'ezier extraction
operators~\cite{borden2011extraction}, needed when a structured IGA mesh is impractical to generate
automatically (as with trimmed geometry), reuse libMesh's generic linear-constraint infrastructure:
extraction coefficients read from ExodusII or LS-DYNA files are stored as constraint rows relating
spline-basis degrees of freedom to element-local nodes, so no IGA-specific parallel code path is
required.

\textbf{Meshing and I/O.} The \texttt{MeshTetInterface} abstract base class unifies libMesh's
interfaces to the bundled TetGen and Netgen tetrahedralizers, giving applications a single API for
generating volume meshes from boundary surfaces. Mesh I/O includes parallel ExodusII/Nemesis, VTK,
Abaqus, Gmsh, STL, and NetCDF support. The element library has added high-order elements
(\texttt{Tri7}, \texttt{Tet14}, \texttt{Prism20}/\texttt{Prism21}, \texttt{Pyramid18}), shell
elements (\texttt{QUADSHELL4}, \texttt{TRISHELL3}, \texttt{QUADSHELL9}), mixed-dimensional meshes,
and generic element-quality metrics (aspect ratio, minimum/maximum angle, and scaled Jacobian).
The \texttt{VariationalMeshSmoother} improves mesh quality by minimizing the distortion--dilation
metric of Branets and Carey~\cite{branets2005smoothing}. It is itself implemented as a libMesh
\texttt{FEMSystem}, with the metric's gradient as the residual and its Hessian as the Jacobian,
and so inherits libMesh's parallel nonlinear solvers. It smooths first- and second-order
edges, triangles, quadrilaterals, tetrahedra, prisms, pyramids, and hexahedra, including
mixed-element meshes, can untangle
some inverted meshes, and can preserve boundaries between subdomains.

\textbf{Parallel infrastructure.} Parallel communication is now implemented in TIMPI, a required
dependency that began as libMesh-internal code and was split into its own independently versioned
and tested repository in 2019. TIMPI offers interchangeable strategies for push/pull-style parallel
data exchange, selected through the \texttt{SyncType} enumeration (\texttt{NBX},
\texttt{ALLTOALL\_COUNTS}, \texttt{SENDRECEIVE}). The \texttt{NBX} strategy implements the
nonblocking dynamic sparse data exchange algorithm of Hoefler et al.~\cite{hoefler2010nbx}, which
avoids the all-to-all communication and a priori knowledge of message counts that earlier, simpler
schemes required. Every parallel libMesh object can be constructed on an arbitrary MPI
communicator, so independent simulations can share one process pool; partitioners accept
per-element weights for load balancing; and variable groups store degree-of-freedom indices
compactly for systems with many identically discretized variables.

\textbf{Mesh data structures.} Every mesh is either a \texttt{ReplicatedMesh}, in which each
processor stores the full mesh, or a \texttt{DistributedMesh}, in which each processor stores
only its own elements plus the ghosted elements determined by the ghosting functors above. The two
share the \texttt{MeshBase} interface, so application code, I/O, and adaptive refinement are
written once for both, and both are exercised in continuous integration. Distributed meshes
remove the per-processor memory bound on mesh size; replicated meshes avoid communication in
mesh-wide operations on more modest meshes.

\textbf{Discretization methods and adaptivity.} Vector-valued finite element families
(\texttt{LAGRANGE\_VEC} for $H^1$, \texttt{NEDELEC\_ONE} for $H(\mathrm{curl})$, \texttt{RAVIART\_THOMAS} for
$H(\mathrm{div})$, and their discontinuous counterparts) complement the scalar field families, and
variables may be restricted to subsets of mesh subdomains. The $C^1$-continuous Clough--Tocher
and Hermite families and Loop subdivision surface elements support fourth-order problems such as
plate and shell bending, and infinite elements extend a bounded mesh to model wave propagation
and other problems on unbounded domains. Periodic boundaries, Dirichlet
boundaries, and general heterogeneous linear constraints share the constraint machinery that
enforces continuity at hanging nodes in adaptively refined meshes. The certified reduced-basis model order reduction capability -- offline/online
reduced-basis construction, the empirical interpolation method for nonaffine operators, and the
successive constraint method for stability-factor lower bounds -- is the numerical core of
Akselos's commercial reduced-order-modeling products. Adjoint-based, goal-oriented error
estimation and $hp$-adaptive refinement strategies extend the flux-jump and patch-recovery
indicators to quantity-of-interest-driven adaptivity. Projections onto finite element
spaces -- of solutions between refined and coarsened meshes, of analytic or user-supplied
functions such as initial conditions, and the construction of projection matrices -- share the
\texttt{GenericProjector} class template. It interpolates values at vertices, then projects onto
edges, sides, and interiors in turn, which preserves inter-element continuity for $C^0$ and $C^1$
families. Its input and output functors are template
parameters, so new data sources and destinations reuse the same algorithm, which supports distributed- and
shared-memory parallelism.

\textbf{System assembly.} libMesh's generic numeric types (\texttt{TypeVector},
\texttt{TypeTensor}, \texttt{VectorValue}, \texttt{TensorValue}, \texttt{DenseVector}, and
\texttt{DenseMatrix}) are templated to work transparently with the dual-number type from the
bundled MetaPhysicL submodule. Downstream codes, notably MOOSE~\cite{lindsay2021metaphysicl}, can
therefore build exact-derivative Jacobian assembly without any
automatic-differentiation-specific code path in libMesh itself. The \texttt{FEMSystem} framework
assembles residuals and Jacobians element by element, using distributed- and shared-memory parallelism, from
user-supplied physics kernels, and couples them to first- and second-order time integrators
(including Newmark); \texttt{OptimizationSystem} interfaces to the TAO and NLopt optimization
libraries. \texttt{EigenSystem} and \texttt{CondensedEigenSystem} solve standard and generalized
eigenproblems through SLEPc, with constrained degrees of freedom (e.g., Dirichlet boundaries)
removed from the operators. On the solver side, libMesh exposes PETSc field-split and geometric
multigrid preconditioning built from its mesh hierarchy, and it supplies the discrete gradient
and curl operators and vertex coordinates required by hypre's auxiliary-space Maxwell (AMS) and
divergence (ADS) solvers~\cite{kolev2009ams,kolev2012ads}, giving scalable preconditioners for the
$H(\mathrm{curl})$ and $H(\mathrm{div})$ systems discretized with the N\'ed\'elec and
Raviart--Thomas families. Finally, the generic numeric types are also usable inside Kokkos
kernels, and a set of Kokkos vector and tensor operations lets downstream codes run
element-level computations on GPUs using the same types as their CPU code.

\textbf{Software quality.} Every pull request is tested by INL's CIVET continuous integration
system, which builds libMesh with the minimum supported and current GCC and Clang compilers on
x86-64 Linux and Apple Silicon macOS, treating compiler warnings as errors, and runs the 79
examples and the CppUnit suite with code-coverage reporting. The tests run with both replicated and distributed meshes, on 1 to
16 MPI processes and with mixed MPI and threaded parallelism, and in configurations with complex
scalars, 32-bit PETSc indices, and deprecated code disabled. Changes merged to the development
branch are further tested with adaptivity, exceptions, MPI, or other optional features disabled;
in debug mode on up to 16 processes; with alternative TIMPI synchronization strategies; under
Valgrind and Clang sanitizers. Every pull request also builds
MOOSE against the proposed libMesh and runs the MOOSE framework and module test suites, in
serial, in parallel, with distributed meshes, and with checkpoint recovery. Many unit tests repeat
each check on randomly permuted, rotated, and skewed elements, catching errors that depend on node
ordering or element orientation. Deprecated interfaces emit warnings before they are removed, and
a \texttt{--disable-deprecated} build turns those warnings into errors so applications can find
deprecated calls ahead of time. libMesh builds with Autoconf, Automake, and Libtool; a single
\texttt{configure} invocation detects optional dependencies and can build optimized, debug, and
other variants side by side, and the installed \texttt{libmesh-config} script and
\texttt{pkg-config} files give downstream codes the matching compiler and linker flags.

\item Sample code snippets analysis

The following excerpt, adapted from libMesh's \texttt{introduction\_ex4} example, solves a
Poisson problem on a distributed mesh of the square $[-1,1]^2$ with Dirichlet boundary conditions
taken from an analytic function. It shows the core workflow shared by all libMesh applications: a
mesh is built, an \texttt{EquationSystems} object holds a system and its variables, constraints are
attached to the system's \texttt{DofMap}, and the system is initialized and solved:

\begin{verbatim}
Mesh mesh(init.comm());
MeshTools::Generation::build_square(mesh, 15, 15,
                                    -1., 1., -1., 1., QUAD9);

EquationSystems equation_systems(mesh);
auto & system =
  equation_systems.add_system<LinearImplicitSystem>("Poisson");
const auto u_var = system.add_variable("u", SECOND, LAGRANGE);
system.attach_assemble_function(assemble_poisson);

AnalyticFunction<> exact_solution_object(exact_solution_wrapper);
DirichletBoundary dirichlet_bc({0, 1, 2, 3}, {u_var},
                               exact_solution_object);
system.get_dof_map().add_dirichlet_boundary(dirichlet_bc);

equation_systems.init();
system.solve();
\end{verbatim}

The same code runs serially or in parallel. The full example reads the variable's order and
family from the command line, so one executable solves the problem with, for instance,
\texttt{HIERARCHIC} or \texttt{BERNSTEIN} bases in place of \texttt{LAGRANGE}. The hybridized discretizations of Section 2.2 are declared with the same
\texttt{add\_variable} calls using the discontinuous and \texttt{SIDE\_HIERARCHIC} families.

\item Impact

libMesh is used across academic, national-laboratory, and commercial settings. It is the long-time
discretization and mesh backend of the MOOSE framework~\cite{harbour2025moose}, and through MOOSE
underlies a large ecosystem of nuclear-engineering, materials, geoscience, and fusion applications
developed by MOOSE's own user community. These include INL's BISON for nuclear fuel
performance~\cite{williamson2021bison}, Griffin for reactor physics~\cite{wang2025griffin},
Pronghorn for reactor thermal hydraulics~\cite{novak2021pronghorn}, and Sockeye for heat
pipes~\cite{hansel2024sockeye}; Argonne National Laboratory's SAM for safety analysis of advanced
non-light-water reactors~\cite{hu2025sam}; and Cardinal~\cite{novak2022cardinal}, which couples
MOOSE to the NekRS computational fluid dynamics and OpenMC Monte Carlo transport codes. These applications rely on
libMesh's mesh data structures, finite element and finite volume discretizations, adaptivity, and
parallel infrastructure, from desktop runs to tens of thousands of processes. Commercially, Akselos builds its reduced-order-modeling
and digital-twin products directly on libMesh. In academia, libMesh supports research codes such
as IBAMR~\cite{griffith2017ibfe} and ongoing research into finite element discretizations:
hybridizable discontinuous Galerkin methods and isogeometric
analysis on trimmed geometry are both active areas of development within the library. Its
parallel communication layer, TIMPI, is independently versioned and tested, so other parallel
scientific codes can reuse it without depending on the rest of libMesh.

\item Conclusions

libMesh has been developed to be an effective foundation for downstream multiphysics simulation:
it supplies discretization, adaptivity, and parallel infrastructure while leaving the physics to
applications. Since its 2006 description, that foundation has grown to include finite volume
support on general polyhedral meshes, isogeometric analysis on structured and trimmed geometry,
hybridized DG discretizations, and supporting infrastructure for
parallel communication, automatic differentiation, and model order reduction. These capabilities reflect two
decades of contributions from a broad developer community. Several, including native polyhedral
elements and refinements to the B\'ezier extraction machinery, were added or substantially revised
within the past year, evidence that libMesh continues to evolve with the needs of its
national-laboratory, academic, and commercial users.

\end{enumerate}
